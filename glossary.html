Architectural Terms

These are the words the brochure uses to describe the houses. Tap any underlined word as you read the tour, or browse the whole list here.

Styles and periods

Queen Anne
The most exuberant style of the late 1800s, built for variety and surprise. Look for an irregular shape, a tower or bay pushing out of one corner, a porch that turns the corner with it, and several different wall textures on the same house.
Neoclassical Revival
The style that swept the country after the 1893 world's fair in Chicago, borrowing columns, pediments, and symmetry from ancient Greece and Rome. In Oakwood it was often added on top of an older Queen Anne house, so you will see both on one facade.
Italianate
A mid-1800s style loosely modeled on Italian villas. Look for tall, narrow windows with arched tops, a low-pitched roof, and rows of decorative brackets under wide eaves.
Second Empire
A style named for the France of Napoleon III and easiest to spot by its mansard roof, frequently set on a central tower.
Craftsman
An early-1900s style that shows how a house is put together instead of hiding it: rafter ends left exposed, sturdy tapered porch posts on brick piers, deep eaves, and plain, solid materials.
Bungalow
A low, wide house of one or one-and-a-half stories with a broad front porch under a low roof — the everyday house of the Craftsman era.
Gothic Revival
A style borrowed from medieval churches, with steep gables, pointed arches, and heavy stonework.
Colonial Revival
A return to the look of early American colonial building — symmetrical fronts, shutters, and classical doorways — popular from about 1900 onward.
Spanish Colonial Revival
A 1920s style drawing on the Spanish missions of the Southwest, and a favorite for gas stations and storefronts of that decade. Look for stucco walls, red tile roofs, and arched openings.
Dutch Colonial Revival
The version of Colonial Revival built under a gambrel roof, whose double slope makes the upper floor roomy enough to live in.
Victorian
A catchall for the decorated houses of the late 1800s. The Society uses "Mid-Victorian" for the local, simpler versions built roughly between 1850 and 1890.
Vernacular
The building tradition of a particular region rather than a national style — the forms local carpenters already knew, repeated and adapted. Most of Oakwood's older houses are vernacular before they are anything else.
Prairie Style
An early-1900s style built around horizontal lines: a low roof, wide overhangs, and windows set in groups rather than singly.
Chinoiserie say “sheen-WAH-zuh-ree”
European decoration imitating Chinese design. On an Oakwood porch it means a railing of crisscrossed flat slats, a pattern the Society notes was popular in Raleigh.

House shapes

Triple-A house
A house with three gables — one at each end, plus a third in the middle of the front. The center gable throws rainwater off to the sides instead of onto whoever is walking up to the door. Very common in North Carolina after 1880.
Gable-front-and-wing
An L-shaped house. About a third of the front is the gable end, the other two-thirds is the wing, and the porch tucks into the corner in front of the wing.
Side-hall plan
A house with its hallway running down one side and the rooms lined up along the other. Narrow and deep, which suited a town lot better than a wide country house did.
Shotgun house
A one-story house one room wide and several rooms deep, with no hallway. The name comes from the idea that a shot fired through the front door would pass through every room and out the back.
Porte-cochere say “port-koh-SHAIR”
A roof extending from the house over the driveway, so passengers could get out of a carriage or car without standing in the rain.
Carriage house
A small building behind the house that sheltered the carriage and horses — the ancestor of the garage.
Duplex
One building divided into two separate homes, often built to look like a single large house.

Roofs

Gable
The triangle of wall left under the two slopes of a roof. Oakwood builders treated the gable as a display case, filling it with shingles, vents, and carved ornament.
Mansard roof say “MAN-sard”
A roof with four steep sides and a nearly flat top, which turns the attic into a full floor. Windows poke out through the steep part. The signature of the Second Empire style.
Gambrel roof
A roof whose sides each break into two slopes, gentle on top and steep below. The barn-roof shape.
Hip roof
A roof with four sloping sides and no gable.
Gable on hip
A small gable riding above a hip roof, so the house gets a decorated triangle without giving up the hipped shape.
Pyramidal roof
A hip roof whose four sides rise to meet at a single point.
Dormer
A window standing upright in the slope of a roof, with a little roof of its own.
Shed dormer
A dormer with one flat slope instead of a peak, which lets it run much wider and light a whole upper room.
Eaves
The lower edge of the roof, where it overhangs the wall. Most of a house's decorative brackets and moldings hang here.
Cornice
The molded band running along the top of a wall where it meets the roof.
Finial
An ornament capping a peak, tower, or post — often a turned wooden spike or an iron point.
Cresting
A low decorative iron railing running along the ridge of a roof, purely for looks.
Parapet
A low wall carried up past the edge of the roof, hiding the roofline behind it.
Witch's hat
The steep cone of a roof over a round tower or a rounded porch corner, usually tipped with a finial.
Ogee say “OH-jee”
An S-curve. A double-ogee tower roof swells outward and then draws back in, giving the tower a bell shape.
Standing-seam roof
A metal roof made of long panels joined by raised, folded seams that run straight down the slope. The seams are the ridges you can see from the sidewalk.
Terne metal say “TURN”
Sheet steel coated in a tin-and-lead alloy, used for roofs from the late 1800s. It has a dull finish rather than a bright one, and repainted faithfully it can last more than a century.
Slate
Roofing split from stone into thin tiles. Oakwood's slate roofs are often laid in patterns, with bands of different shapes.

Porches, posts, and trim

Turned post
A post shaped on a lathe as it spins, so it comes out ringed with beads and bulges instead of flat-sided. The most characteristic woodwork of the period.
Chamfered say “CHAM-ferd”
With the sharp corners cut off at an angle. A chamfered porch post reads as eight-sided rather than square.
Battered
Tapered — wider at the bottom than at the top. Craftsman porch posts are usually battered and set on brick piers.
Sawnwork
Flat boards cut into decorative patterns with a scroll saw, then applied to porches and gables. Cheaper than carving, and the reason so many modest houses got fancy trim.
Spindlework
A band of small turned spindles, like a row of little table legs, set in a row beneath a porch roof.
Fretwork
Openwork ornament cut from thin wood in an interlocking pattern.
Frieze say “FREEZ”
The flat horizontal band running just under the eaves or a porch roof, often filled with ornament.
Bracket
A decorative support tucked under the eaves or a porch roof. Italianate houses use them in pairs, spaced along the wall.
Modillion say “moh-DIL-yun”
A bracket laid on its side under the edge of a roof, usually scrolled — larger and bolder than a dentil.
Dentil molding say “DEN-til”
A row of small square blocks under the eaves, like a row of teeth.
Mutule say “MYOO-tyool”
A flat block on the underside of a Greek Doric cornice. It does the same job as a modillion, but plainer and squared off.
Balustrade say “BAL-uh-strayd”
A railing held up by a row of short posts, called balusters. Look for them along porch edges and roof lines.
Colonnette say “kah-luh-NET”
A small, slender column, frequently set in pairs on a porch.
Fluted
Cut with shallow vertical grooves running up a column.
Tuscan column
The plainest of the classical columns: a smooth shaft with a simple round top and no carving.
Ionic column say “eye-ON-ik”
A classical column whose top curls into a pair of scrolls, like a rolled scroll seen end-on.
Capital
The decorated top of a column. The shape of the capital is what tells you which classical order you are looking at.
Pedestal
The block a column or post stands on, raising it off the porch floor.
Pilaster say “pih-LAS-ter”
A flattened column attached to the wall — decoration rather than support. Often placed on either side of a front door.
Pediment
The triangular top of the front of a Greek or Roman temple, borrowed here to crown a doorway, a window, or a gable.
Portico say “POR-tih-koh”
A porch held up by columns, marking the entrance to the house.
Entablature say “en-TAB-luh-chur”
The horizontal band a classical building carries above its columns. It is made of a cornice and a wide flat board, and it continues all the way around the eaves.
Wraparound porch
A porch that keeps going around a corner of the house and down a second side.
Exposed rafter tails
The ends of the roof rafters left visible past the wall instead of boxed in behind trim. A Craftsman signature.
Consumption porch
A second-story sleeping balcony, from the years when doctors prescribed fresh night air for tuberculosis, then called consumption.

Windows and doors

Bay window
A window that projects out from the wall, making a small alcove in the room inside.
Cutaway bay
A bay whose front corners are angled back instead of square, so it reads as half an octagon. The most common bay in Oakwood.
Bow window
A bay window that curves in a smooth arc rather than turning corners.
Palladian window say “puh-LAY-dee-un”
A group of three windows: a tall arched one in the middle with a shorter square-topped window on each side.
Transom
A window set directly above a door, there to light the hall before houses had electricity.
Fanlight
A half-round transom above a door, with bars spreading out like the ribs of a fan.
Sidelights
The narrow windows flanking a front door on either side.
Lunette say “loo-NET”
A half-moon window, usually tucked into a gable or over a door.
Rose window
A round window with spokes radiating from the center, borrowed from church architecture. Also called a wheel window.
Quatrefoil say “KWA-truh-foyl”
A four-lobed shape, like a stylized four-leaf clover. The quatrefoil attic vent is the symbol of the Oakwood neighborhood.
Sunburst
A carved fan of rays spreading from a center point, usually set high in a gable.
Leaded glass
Small panes of glass held together by narrow strips of lead, which let a window carry a pattern.
Beveled glass
Clear glass with its edges ground at an angle, so the border catches the light and throws rainbows across the hall in direct sun.
Stained glass
Colored glass set into a design. In Oakwood it often appears as a border of small colored panes framing a plain center pane.
Diamond-paned
Glass divided into diamonds instead of rectangles.
Jack arch
A flat arch of wedge-shaped bricks spanning the top of a window or door. It looks like a straight row of brick but carries weight the way a curved arch does.
Door surround
The whole frame of trim around a doorway, counting any transom and sidelights.

Walls and materials

German siding
Wood siding with a scooped hollow along the top of each board, which casts a deep shadow line across the wall. Also sold as drop or novelty siding.
Scalloped shingles
Wood shingles cut with rounded ends and laid in rows, so the wall reads as bands of half-circles.
Fish-scale shingles
Scalloped shingles laid in overlapping rows so the surface looks like the scales of a fish.
Half-timbering
Dark boards applied over light stucco in a pattern of exposed framing, borrowed from English medieval building. Decoration here, not structure.
Stucco
A hard plaster coating spread over an exterior wall.
Pressed tin
Sheet metal stamped into raised patterns and used for roofs, ceilings, and storefront cornices — an affordable stand-in for carved stone or wood.
Gneiss say “NICE”
A hard rock whose light and dark minerals have been squeezed into visible stripes by heat and pressure. The banding is what tells it apart from granite.
Carriage stone
A stone block set at the curb in line with the front walk, used as a step for climbing in and out of a carriage. Several survive on Oakwood streets.
